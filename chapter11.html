Cache Eviction
Policies

Your cache is full. A new item arrives. Which one gets kicked out? The answer depends on the eviction policy — and most people pick the wrong one.

LRU LFU FIFO TTL SIEVE
🚨

The Eviction Problem

Cache memory is finite. When it's full, every new entry requires removing an existing one. The question: which one?

Your cache has 4 slots. All full. A new item E arrives.

Who gets evicted?

The wrong choice means your most useful data gets thrown out. Cache hit rate drops. Database load spikes.

Eviction seems like a small decision. But at scale, the difference between a 95% and 85% cache hit rate can mean 10x more database load. The eviction policy determines what stays and what goes — and every policy makes a different bet on what you'll need next.

The real-world version: Your desk has limited space. You can keep 10 books on it. The 11th arrives. Do you remove the one you haven't touched in weeks (LRU)? The one you've only opened once (LFU)? The one that arrived first (FIFO)? Or the one with a due date that passed (TTL)? Each strategy makes a different assumption about what's valuable.
⚙

The Eviction Policies

Six policies, each with a different philosophy on what to keep and what to discard.
🕒
LRU — Least Recently Used
Redis • Memcached • Linux Page Cache • Most systems

Evict the item that hasn't been accessed for the longest time. The assumption: if you haven't used it recently, you probably won't need it soon.

Every read and write updates the item's position. The least recently touched item sits at the back of the queue. When eviction is needed, the back of the queue goes first.

Strengths

Good default. Works well for most access patterns. Intuitive. Battle-tested across decades of production systems.

Tradeoffs

Every read updates access order — CPU overhead at high throughput. A one-time full scan (like a batch job) can flush your entire hot working set.

📈
LFU — Least Frequently Used
Redis (allkeys-lfu) • CDNs • Highly skewed workloads

Evict the item that's been accessed the fewest times overall. The assumption: items accessed many times are more valuable than items accessed rarely.

Each item carries a counter. Every access increments it. The item with the lowest count gets evicted first.

Strengths

Excellent for skewed workloads where a small set of keys gets most of the traffic. Keeps the truly hot data around.

Tradeoffs

A once-viral key that's now cold keeps its high count forever — it never gets evicted. Needs a decay mechanism (Redis uses probabilistic aging). More memory per key (counter storage).

📋
FIFO — First In, First Out
Simple caches • Twitter's Twemcache • Kernel buffers

Evict the oldest item — the one that was inserted first. No tracking of access patterns. Pure queue: first in, first out.

No per-read metadata updates. No access counters. No reordering. Just a pointer to the head of the queue.

Strengths

Simplest possible implementation. Lowest CPU overhead. No per-read bookkeeping. Twitter's research showed it performs surprisingly close to LRU.

Tradeoffs

Ignores access patterns entirely. A frequently accessed item gets evicted just because it's old. Works best when access patterns are roughly uniform.

⏰
TTL — Time To Live
DNS • Session tokens • API responses • CDN edges

Every item gets a countdown timer when it enters the cache. When the timer hits zero, the item expires — regardless of how popular or recently accessed it is.

TTL is less about choosing what to evict and more about ensuring freshness. It's the only policy that guarantees data staleness is bounded.

Strengths

Predictable. Guarantees maximum staleness. Perfect for data that changes on a known schedule (DNS records, session tokens, config).

Tradeoffs

Doesn't consider popularity or access patterns. If many keys share the same TTL, they all expire at once — causing a cache stampede (Chapter 10).

🔄
MRU — Most Recently Used
Niche • Sequential scan workloads • Database buffer pools

The opposite of LRU: evict the most recently used item. Sounds counterintuitive, but it's optimal for sequential scan patterns where you process each item once and never return to it.

Strengths

Optimal for sequential access patterns. Prevents a full table scan from flushing the entire cache.

Tradeoffs

Terrible for most workloads. Only useful in specific scenarios like database full-table scans or batch processing pipelines.

🎯
SIEVE — The New Contender
NSDI 2024 • Research • Next-gen cache systems

Published at NSDI 2024. Uses one pointer and one bit per object. Simpler than LRU, better hit ratios in benchmarks. The pointer scans the cache looking for items with the bit set to 0 (not recently accessed). Items that have been accessed get their bit set to 1 — they survive one pass.

Think of it as a lazy, forgiving FIFO: items get one chance to prove they're still useful. If they've been accessed since the last scan, they survive. If not, they're evicted.

Strengths

Lower overhead than LRU. Better hit ratios than FIFO. One bit per object. No linked list reordering on every read. The next evolution of cache eviction.

Tradeoffs

New — not yet widely adopted in production. Less battle-tested than LRU. Performance advantages depend on workload characteristics.

The practical guidance:

Starting a new system? LRU. It's the safe default.
Highly skewed traffic (celebrity profiles, viral posts)? LFU.
Need maximum throughput with minimal CPU? FIFO.
Data has a known freshness window? TTL (combine with LRU or FIFO).
Building a new cache from scratch? Look at SIEVE.

In practice, most production systems combine TTL with another policy. Redis, for example, lets you set both a TTL and an eviction policy (LRU or LFU). The TTL handles freshness. The eviction policy handles memory pressure.

📚

Twitter's Research: The Surprise Finding

153 production cache clusters. 80 TB of trace data. The result nobody expected.
📎
OSDI 2020 — "A Large Scale Analysis of Hundreds of In-Memory Cache Clusters at Twitter"
Juncheng Yang, Yao Yue, K.V. Rashmi

Twitter analyzed 153 production Memcached clusters, collecting over 80 TB of trace data. They compared LRU, FIFO, and other eviction policies across real workloads.

"FIFO often shows similar performance as LRU. LRU often exhibits advantages only when the cache size is severely limited."

This means: at normal cache sizes (where you have reasonable headroom), the simpler FIFO algorithm delivers nearly identical cache hit rates to LRU — with significantly less CPU overhead.

Why does FIFO match LRU?

Real-world access patterns follow Zipfian distribution — a small set of keys (the "head") gets the vast majority of requests. Both LRU and FIFO keep these hot keys in cache because they're constantly being re-inserted or accessed. The eviction policy primarily affects the "tail" keys — the rarely accessed ones. And for those, it barely matters which one you evict.

📈

Zipfian Distribution

~20% of keys serve ~80% of requests. These hot keys survive eviction under any policy. The eviction algorithm only matters for the long tail.

⏰

TTL Dominates

Twitter found that short TTLs make proactive expiration more important than the eviction algorithm. Most items expire before eviction is needed.

💻

CPU Savings

LRU requires reordering a linked list on every read. FIFO doesn't. At millions of ops/sec, the CPU savings from FIFO are significant.

🔍

Size Matters

LRU only outperforms FIFO when cache size is severely limited (<10% of working set). At reasonable sizes, the gap disappears.

The staff-level insight: Don't pick an eviction policy based on intuition. "Evict the least recently used" feels right, but the data shows simpler is often good enough. Profile your access patterns. If your traffic is Zipfian (it almost certainly is), FIFO with TTL might be all you need.

The System Design Interview

"What eviction policy would you use and why?"
"You're designing a cache for a social media feed. The cache is 10 GB. Your working set is 50 GB. What eviction policy do you use?"
✘ THE REJECTED ANSWER
"LRU. It's the standard choice for caching."
Interviewer thinking: "They named the default without reasoning about the workload."
Same question. Better answer.
✔ THE STAFF-LEVEL ANSWER
"Social media feeds have Zipfian access patterns — celebrity accounts and viral posts dominate traffic. I'd use LFU to keep those hot keys cached, since they're accessed far more than the tail. I'd combine it with TTL of 5 minutes to bound staleness — feeds need to show recent content. For the eviction mechanism, Redis's allkeys-lfu with probabilistic aging handles the 'once-viral-now-cold' problem. I'd monitor cache hit rate — if it drops below 90%, either the cache is undersized or the TTL is too aggressive."

When to mention each policy:

🕒

LRU — Safe Default

Use when you don't know the access pattern. Works well for general web apps, session caches, API response caches.

📈

LFU — Skewed Traffic

Use when a small set of keys gets most requests. Social feeds, product catalogs, celebrity profiles.

📋

FIFO — Max Throughput

Mention the Twitter paper. Show you know LRU's overhead and that simpler can be better. Strong signal in interviews.

⏰

TTL — Always Combine

Never use eviction without TTL. TTL handles freshness. Eviction handles memory pressure. They solve different problems.

🎯

Key Takeaways

What to remember about Cache Eviction Policies
🕒

LRU Is Not Always Best

Twitter proved FIFO matches LRU at normal cache sizes. Don't pick LRU by default — pick it because your workload needs it.

📈

Know Your Access Pattern

Zipfian? LFU. Uniform? FIFO. Sequential? MRU. The workload determines the policy, not convention.

⏰

Always Add TTL

Eviction handles memory. TTL handles freshness. Every production cache needs both. They solve different problems.

🎯

Watch SIEVE

One bit per object. Better than LRU in benchmarks. Lower overhead than LFU. The next generation of cache eviction.
